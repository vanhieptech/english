## 0. Format baseline (needed for every part)

### Takeaway
The TOEIC L&R Listening section has 100 questions in about 45 minutes: Part 1 has 6, Part 2 has 25, Part 3 has 39 (13 conversations x 3) and Part 4 has 30 (10 talks x 3). Every recording is played once. No format change after the 2016/2018 revision was found in what this research covered.

### Cited Findings
- Listening is about 45 min and 100 questions. Part 1 Photographs (6): four statements per photo, spoken once and not printed. Part 2 Question-Response (25): three responses, spoken once and not printed. Part 3 Conversations (39): two or three speakers, 3 questions each, questions printed and also spoken. Part 4 Talks (30): 3 questions each. — [IIBC format page](https://www.iibc-global.org/english/toeic/test/lr/about/format.html)
- In Parts 3 and 4, some questions need information from diagrams or other graphics printed in the test book. Part 3 includes conversations with three speakers. — [IIBC format page](https://www.iibc-global.org/english/toeic/test/lr/about/format.html)
- Some older prep materials still describe 10 photos and 30 Part 2 items, which is the pre-2016 format. Learners should avoid those materials or adjust for the difference. — [EF TOEIC listening](https://www.ef.com/wwen/english-tests/toeic/listening/); [open-exam-prep / lumetest search snippets noting conflicting counts](https://lumetest.com/blog/toeic/free-toeic-part-2-practice-questions-question-response)
- Official ETS sample test PDF, for reference: [ETS TOEIC L&R sample test](https://www.mx.ets.org/content/dam/ets-org/pdfs/toeic/toeic-listening-reading-sample-test.pdf)

### Inferences
- About 69% of Listening points come from Parts 3 and 4 (69/100). In a 30-day plan, Part 3/4 drills should get the largest share of time. Part 2 (25%) is the second priority because it is pure listening reflex with no printed text.

## Part 1: distractor patterns, key vocabulary and how to drill it

### Takeaway
Part 1 traps fall into a few families: right noun with wrong verb, sound-alike words, the "being" vs "been" passive trap, objects that are visible but described with an action not happening, and speculation or absolutes. A photo with no people almost always has a state or position answer (has been + V3, is + V3, is + prep phrase), not an action-in-progress answer.

### Cited Findings
- Trap taxonomy from one prep guide: (1) similar-sounding words (walking/working, copy/coffee, cart/card); (2) incorrect combinations, where the noun is right and the verb is wrong or the reverse; (3) overgeneralization; (4) voice/aspect errors, where "being" is used with no visible person and is usually wrong; (5) absolutes (all/every/none) rarely correct; (6) speculation about past/future/feelings; (7) background objects used to build distractors. — [open-exam-prep: Part 1 Photographs](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/photographs)
- The "being vs been" trap is called the most common grammar trap in object-only photos. "The chairs are being set up" is wrong when no one is setting them up, while "The chairs have been set up" (completed state) is right. — [open-exam-prep: Part 1](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/photographs)
- Caveat: "is being + V3" is not automatically wrong. When a person is doing the action, the correct answer may use the object as subject in the present continuous passive. Example pattern: a worker painting a wall, answer "A wall is being painted." — [search summary of Part 1 tips, ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn](https://ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn/tai-lieu-hoc-tap-eng/tactics-for-part-i-toeic-listening-6t1g9)
- When a photo has no people, expect a state or spatial-relationship description. — [ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn Part 1 tips](https://ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn/goc-hoc-tap/some-tips-for-part-i-toeic-listening-test-aoq5k)
- Vocabulary categories to drill: for photos with people, subject, action verb, setting and the object being handled. For object photos, prepositions of place ("stacked on top of", "mounted on the wall") and state words ("unoccupied", "cleared", "fully stocked"). — [open-exam-prep: Part 1](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/photographs)
- Process strategy: use the pause before the audio to classify the photo as people-centric or object-centric, track options with a pencil, and check the verb, not only the noun. — [open-exam-prep: Part 1](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/photographs)
- High-frequency Part 1 grammar patterns and vocabulary list (study note): [knowt TOEIC Part 1 patterns](https://knowt.com/note/b8b95dd7-625f-471c-9f5d-3683b97bd7bb/TOEIC-Part-1--Common-Grammatical-Patter)

### Inferences
- A further trap type is commonly taught, though the sources above do not name it this way: object seen but action wrong. The noun is in the photo, so the option "sounds right." Original example: the photo shows a woman looking at a monitor, and the distractor says "She's turning on the monitor." The rule is to verify the verb first.
- A second commonly taught pattern is "wearing vs putting on." "is wearing" is a state and "is putting on" is an action. Original example: "He's putting on a helmet" is wrong if the helmet is already on. No source fetched for this; flag it as standard prep lore.
- Drill method using parroto.app: run dictation on short clips that describe scenes, such as workplace, street or kitchen videos. Then make an SRS deck of about 60 to 100 Part 1 verbs and positions: lean against, stack, hang, line up, face, reach for, pour, sweep, board, unload, be propped against, be arranged, be displayed, overlook. Shadow each set of 4 statements from a mock test after checking answers. Part 1 is only 6 points, so 2 to 3 days of focused work is probably enough. After that, maintain it through mock tests.
- Contrast pairs for minimal-pair listening drills: being/been, walking/working, copy/coffee, cart/card, plus common items like file/pile and lamp/ramp.

- There is no official ETS breakdown of how often each trap type appears. I found no sourced frequency data.

## Part 2: question-type taxonomy, traps and indirect answers

### Takeaway
Part 2 has 25 items in two broad families. About 60% are WH-questions and about 40% are non-information prompts: yes/no, negative, tag, choice and statement, plus suggestion/request/offer. The biggest traps are repeated or similar-sounding words and answers that fit the wrong question type. Correct answers on harder items are often indirect. No source gives a verified percentage of indirect answers.

### Cited Findings
- Split: about 60% WH/information questions and about 40% non-information (Yes/No, choice, statements). This comes from a prep site, not ETS. — [open-exam-prep: Part 2 info questions](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/question-response-info); [open-exam-prep: Part 2 non-info](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/question-response-non-info)
- Types and correct-answer logic, per prep guide:
  - Yes/No questions start with an auxiliary or modal. The correct answer often leaves out "yes/no" and implies the answer.
  - Negative questions: answer by the facts, not the negative phrasing.
  - Tag questions: the correct answer confirms or corrects the assumption.
  - Choice (A or B): the answer picks A, picks B, picks both, or rejects both and suggests a third option. Responses starting with "Yes/No" are "almost always incorrect distractors" for choice questions.
  - Statements (observation, complaint, news): the correct answer is a natural coworker follow-up, such as an offer to fix the problem.
  - Indirect forms include a polite decline with a reason, and deflecting ("ask X", "I wasn't at the meeting").
  — [open-exam-prep: Part 2 non-info](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/question-response-non-info)
- On higher-difficulty Part 2 items, correct answers are often polite, indirect or non-committal. — [open-exam-prep: Part 2 info](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/toeic/listening-photographs-question-response/question-response-info)
- Options that repeat a word from the question are often traps, and correct answers tend to paraphrase. — [lumetest Part 2 practice](https://lumetest.com/blog/toeic/free-toeic-part-2-practice-questions-question-response); [usingenglish: teaching Part 2](https://www.usingenglish.com/teachers/articles/how-to-teach-toeic-listening-part-two-question-response.html)
- Additional practice items and descriptions: [examenglish Part 2](https://www.examenglish.com/TOEIC/TOEIC_listening_part2a.htm)

### Inferences
- Full working taxonomy (synthesized; the original examples are by the researcher):
  1. WH- (Who/What/When/Where/Why/How/Which/How long/How often/How much). Trap: a "Yes/No" answer to a WH question is wrong. So is an answer to the wrong WH word, such as a place answer to a When question. "When/Where" confusion is a classic trap.
  2. Yes/No and auxiliary questions (Do/Is/Have/Can/Will). The correct answer may skip "yes."
  3. Negative questions ("Didn't you...", "Isn't the...").
  4. Tag questions ("..., isn't it?", "..., right?").
  5. Choice questions ("A or B?").
  6. Suggestion/request/offer: "Why don't we...", "Could you...", "Would you like...", "How about...". Original example: "Why don't we move the meeting to Friday?" The wrong reply treats "Why" as a reason question ("Because it's late"). The right reply is something like "Thursday works better for me."
  7. Statements: complaint, observation or news, with no question mark.
  8. Indirect questions ("Do you know where...?"), which work like WH-questions inside a Yes/No frame.
- Trap taxonomy to teach:
  - (a) repeated word: the same word as the question, but irrelevant;
  - (b) similar sound: copy/coffee, contract/contact, review/view, lunch/launch;
  - (c) related-word association: question about "flight", option mentions "boarding pass" but doesn't answer;
  - (d) wrong question type: Yes/No to a WH-question, or reason to a "Why don't we" suggestion;
  - (e) wrong tense or person: answers in the past about a future plan, or "he" when no man was mentioned;
  - (f) a reply that answers a different WH-word.
- Why indirect answers are correct: Part 2 tests real workplace pragmatics. Native speakers often answer with a reason, a counter-question ("Has the client confirmed yet?"), a deferral ("Let me check the schedule") or "I don't know"-type moves. Direct-keyword answers are easy to fake with distractors, so test designers can hide the right answer behind paraphrase. Strategy: if two options are clearly wrong by trap type, choose the remaining one even if it "sounds off-topic."
- Elimination workflow: (1) catch the first 2 to 3 words (the question word plus auxiliary) because they decide the type; (2) for each option, reject it on trap type rather than hunting for the "right" one; (3) never leave blank, so guess and move on before the next item begins.
- Drill in parroto.app: dictation of only the first 3 to 5 words of question prompts, which trains capture of the question type. Shadow the full Q plus correct A to build a pragmatic reflex. In a mock test review, label each wrong item with its trap code (a-f) to build a personal error log.

- The 60/40 WH vs non-WH split is a prep-site estimate, not ETS.

## Part 3/4: pre-reading, graphics, implied meaning, 3 speakers, paraphrase and recovery

### Takeaway
Parts 3 and 4 reward reading the 3 questions (and graphic) before the audio, predicting where in the recording each answer comes, and matching paraphrase rather than keywords. Graphics and "Why does the speaker say '...'" items are the post-2016 additions. Recovery means letting go immediately and moving to the next question or set.

### Cited Findings
- Question families in Part 3: main topic/purpose, problem, next action/suggestion, inference, and specific details (times, places, numbers, plans, procedures). — [zim.vn: Part 3 question types](https://zim.vn/dang-bai-doan-hoi-thoai-ngan-toeic-listening-part-3)
- Strategy from zim.vn: pre-read the questions and options; note dates, times, quantities, places and names; expect answers to use synonyms or paraphrase rather than the audio's exact words; don't dwell on one question. — [zim.vn Part 3](https://zim.vn/dang-bai-doan-hoi-thoai-ngan-toeic-listening-part-3)
- Part 4 types (voice message, monologue) on zim.vn: [zim.vn Part 4 voice message](https://zim.vn/dang-bai-dang-tin-nhan-thoai-trong-toeic-listening-part-4); [zim.vn Part 4 question types](https://zim.vn/dang-cau-hoi-tin-nhan-thoai-doc-thoai-toeic-listening-part-4)
- Graphic items: the graphic is printed in the test book, and one question in the set asks you to combine it with the audio. These appear in both Parts 3 and 4. — [IIBC format](https://www.iibc-global.org/english/toeic/test/lr/about/format.html); [lumetest: graphic questions in Parts 3-4](https://lumetest.com/blog/toeic/toeic-listening-practice)
- Part 3 conversations can involve three speakers. — [IIBC format](https://www.iibc-global.org/english/toeic/test/lr/about/format.html)
- Question-type introduction for Part 3 (Duy Tan Univ. English faculty): [ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn](https://ktienganh.duytan.edu.vn/bai-viet-suu-tam-0ntt7/an-introduction-to-types-of-questions-in-part-3-of-the-toeic-test-gmdf4)

### Inferences
These are synthesized technique points. Sourcing is thin, so the report writer should present them as standard prep practice, not research-backed.
- Pre-reading: use the directions time for Part 3 and the time while the three questions of the previous set are read aloud. Read only the question stems first, underlining WH-word + subject (man/woman/speaker) + key noun. Skim options only if time allows. Questions usually follow the order of the audio: Q1 early (topic/purpose/problem), Q2 middle (detail/reason), Q3 late (next action/request/what will happen). Typical Q3 signal phrases are "I'll...", "Could you...", "Why don't you...", "Let me...".
- Gender/role tagging: note who each question is about ("What does the woman suggest?"). In 3-speaker conversations, two speakers of the same gender often appear (e.g., "man 1" and "man 2"), and questions then refer to roles or names, such as "What does Mr. X say?". Tag roles when the speakers introduce themselves.
- Graphics: before the audio, find the column or category in the graphic that is not in the answer options. Options typically list one dimension (e.g., floor numbers) and the audio mentions the other (e.g., department names), so the answer requires a cross-reference. Original example: the graphic is a schedule of sessions × rooms and the options are room numbers. The speaker says "I'll attend the session right after lunch," so you must find that time slot and read across to the room.
- Implied-meaning ("Why does the man say, 'That's the third time this week'?"): the quoted line is printed, so wait for it in the audio and focus on the 1 to 2 sentences before and after it. The literal meaning is usually a distractor, and the answer is the function: complaint, refusal, offer, surprise or concern. Original example: "I've got a client call at two" usually means "I can't join the meeting," not "I have a phone."
- Paraphrase patterns: the options rarely repeat audio words. Common types are synonym (purchase → buy), general for specific (laptop → device; Tuesday at 3 → later this week), and grammar change (it was delayed → it arrived late). An option that repeats an audio word verbatim deserves suspicion, the mirror of the Part 2 repeated-word trap.
- Recovery after missing an answer: (1) mark your best guess immediately and do not try to replay it in memory; (2) note that answers for Q1 to Q3 come in order, so if you hear Q3's answer you have missed Q2, so guess Q2 now; (3) use the time while the next set's questions are read aloud to pre-read the next set, because this is where most score loss cascades; (4) never sacrifice the next set to rescue the current one.
- Part 4 talk types are predictable genres: phone message, announcement, broadcast/traffic/weather report, tour guide, speech/introduction, advertisement, meeting excerpt. The first sentence usually reveals the genre and purpose. Train genre recognition with 2 to 3 transcripts per type.
- parroto.app use: for each mock test Part 3/4 set reviewed, (a) dictate the 1 to 2 sentences that contained each answer, (b) underline the paraphrase pair between transcript and option, and add the pair to SRS, (c) shadow the whole talk once with pronunciation scoring.

### Gaps
- No official ETS data on the proportion of graphic and intent items. Prep folklore commonly cites roughly 3 graphic plus 2 intent items in Part 3, and about 2 + 3 in Part 4. These counts were not verified in any fetched source, so present them as "approximately, per prep sites" or omit them.
- Vietnamese prep pages specifically on intent and graphic questions were not found or fetched (the zim.vn general Part 3 article does not cover them).

## Evidence on dictation, shadowing and intensive vs extensive listening

### Takeaway
The best-supported approaches for short time frames are bottom-up decoding work (dictation, shadowing on known content) plus strategy instruction. A listening strategy-instruction meta-analysis gives d = 0.69. Shadowing works best on content already understood. Extensive listening helps in the long run, but its measured effects, including on TOEIC, are small and slow. So for 30 days, a mostly intensive approach with some extensive listening is justified.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis: listening strategy instruction has a medium effect on L2 listening comprehension, d = 0.69 (45 studies, 51 samples), with effects varying by moderators. — [Dalman & Plonsky 2022, Language Teaching Research (repository)](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-listening-strategy-instructi/); [Bohrium record, DOI 10.1177/13621688211072981](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-listening-strategy-instruction-a-meta-analysis/812560549266063361-6437)
- Vandergrift & Tafaghodtari (2010, Language Learning 60(2)) used a metacognitive pedagogical cycle (predict → listen → verify → evaluate). In secondary summaries, less-skilled listeners gained the most. The control group also improved. Sources disagree on study length (10 vs 14 weeks), and a later study (Wang 2016) did not replicate the advantage. — [Univ. of Alberta ERA thesis citing the study](https://era.library.ualberta.ca/items/a7f0bfc8-90b4-4d0d-badd-fc4e69ae537a); [NAU paper on metacognitive strategies](https://nau.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/117/TEACHING_METACOGNITIVE_STRATEGIES_LISTENING_PERFORMANCE.pdf); [Frontiers in Psychology 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1285059)
- Field: L2 listening failures often come from decoding (phoneme recognition, word boundaries, lexical matching) rather than comprehension strategy. He proposes a diagnostic approach: find which bottom-up process fails and design short targeted micro-exercises. Recommended activities include dictating short sentences with connected-speech features and listening cloze. — [Cambridge: Field, Listening in the Language Classroom, ch. "Decoding sounds, syllables and words"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/listening-in-the-language-classroom/decoding-sounds-syllables-and-words/769B9EAA8CA7795FD55B0A507896AD29); [IH Journal: developing word recognition sub-skills](https://ihworld.com/ih-journal/issues/issue-50/developing-word-recognition-sub-skills-in-listening); [CATESOL/eScholarship study](https://escholarship.org/content/qt91c921b9/qt91c921b9.pdf?t=sl5cbt); [Oxford: bottom-up decoding](https://teachingenglishwithoxford.oup.com/2016/01/20/bottom-up-decoding-listening/)
- Shadowing: Hamada (2016) and Tamai (2005) are cited as showing shadowing improves L2 listening. The mechanism is better bottom-up processing, meaning perception and recognition of words. — [JALT Journal 47.1](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/jj47.1-art1.pdf); [Hamada 2016, "Shadowing: What is it? How to use it", RELC Journal](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0033688218771380)
- Pre- vs post-shadowing (Hamada 2014): only learners who shadowed after studying the content (known text) showed statistically significant listening gains. — [JALT: effectiveness of pre- and post-shadowing](https://jalt-publications.org/node/3537); [The Language Teacher 38.1](https://jalt-publications.org/sites/default/files/pdf-article/38.1tlt_art1.pdf)
- Hamada also studied how text difficulty affects shadowing progress and metacognitive strategy use. — [Second Language (J-STAGE)](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/secondlanguage/8/0/8_27/_article/-char/en)
- Caveat: no summative review of shadowing exists yet, and most studies come from Japanese classrooms. — [Springer open-access paper](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s40862-017-0039-6.pdf)
- Dictation evidence is positive but mixed.
  - Kiany & Shiramiry (2002) found significantly higher gains with frequent dictation (11 exercises in a term) for elementary learners. — [TESL Canada Journal](https://teslcanadajournal.ca/index.php/tesl/article/download/938/757/965)
  - Nam & Seong (2009, Korea) found no significant between-group difference, though the dictation group improved more. — [KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200917357306519.pdf)
  - A reading-listening integrated dictation outperformed traditional dictation. — [Chinese Journal of Applied Linguistics (De Gruyter)](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/CJAL-2021-0026/html)
  - A Vietnamese 2025 quasi-experiment (Vu & Hoang) reports efficacy for bottom-up listening. — [HCMC Open University Journal of Science](https://journalofscience.ou.edu.vn/index.php/soci-en/article/view/3924)
- Extensive listening (EL): Renandya & Farrell (2011) argue for large amounts of comprehensible input. Critics accept EL as a support component but not as the main one. Chang & Renandya (2023) found significant TOEIC gains from EL, but with small effect sizes. Empirical support for EL is described as limited. — [Unisma JREALL: multidimensional effects of EL](https://riset.unisma.ac.id/index.php/JREALL/article/view/21190); [Aston: "Thoughts on L2 listening pedagogy" (critique)](https://research.aston.ac.uk/en/publications/thoughts-on-l2-listening-pedagogy/); [Cambridge Language Teaching](https://cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/S0261444822000507)

### Inferences
- For 30 days, the evidence points to a cycle on test-like audio: (1) listen and answer under test conditions; (2) check, using the metacognitive verify/evaluate step; (3) dictate the parts you missed, which is Field-style diagnosis of decoding errors; (4) shadow the same audio after you understand it, which follows Hamada's post-shadowing finding. This maps directly onto parroto.app features: mock test → dictation with AI correction → shadowing with pronunciation scoring → vocab SRS for paraphrase pairs and Part 1 verbs.
- Shadowing should be done on known material, so with a transcript or after dictation, not as a first exposure to unseen audio.
- Extensive listening should be a light supplement (commute, at natural speed, business-themed podcasts or videos). It is not the main lever in 30 days.

### Gaps
- No study specifically measures dictation or shadowing effects on TOEIC Listening scores within 30 days. Effect sizes for Hamada's and Kiany's studies were not extracted. Full texts were not opened.

## Accents in TOEIC (US, UK, Canada, Australia) and how to train them

### Takeaway
TOEIC Listening uses American, British, Canadian and Australian speakers. That is confirmed here only by a secondary source (EF), because ETS pages failed to load. Canadian is close to American, so the main adjustment for Vietnamese learners trained on US audio is British and Australian vowels, non-rhotic "r", and t-pronunciation.

### Cited Findings
- Recordings can use American, British, Canadian or Australian accents. — [EF: TOEIC listening](https://www.ef.com/wwen/english-tests/toeic/listening/)
- The IIBC format page does not specify narrator accents. — [IIBC format](https://www.iibc-global.org/english/toeic/test/lr/about/format.html)

### Inferences
- ETS's own handbook is commonly cited as listing those four varieties, with New Zealand sometimes grouped with Australia. The report writer should cite EF and suggest checking the ETS sample test/handbook.
- Training: in parroto.app, deliberately select real videos with British and Australian speakers (e.g., UK/AU news or business channels) for 1 in every 3 dictation sessions, and log accent-specific mishearings. Examples: "schedule" /ʃ/ in UK; non-rhotic "car park", "water" /ˈwɔːtə/; AU "day" sounding near "die"; "can't" /kɑːnt/ vs US /kænt/. Shadow non-US clips to build perception, though the goal is recognition, not changing one's own accent.

### Gaps
- Official proportion of each accent per test is not published, as far as this research found.

## Recommended daily minutes and drill sequencing for 30 days

### Takeaway
No research specifies minutes for TOEIC Listening in 30 days. A defensible plan, inferred from the evidence above, is about 60 to 90 minutes per day of listening work in this order: diagnostic → Part 2 and Part 1 reflex → Part 3/4 technique → full mocks with an error-log cycle. Most time goes to intensive dictation and shadowing on test audio.

### Cited Findings
- Strategy instruction (predict/verify/evaluate) has a medium effect (d = 0.69), supporting explicit technique teaching rather than practice volume alone. — [Dalman & Plonsky 2022](https://experts.nau.edu/en/publications/the-effectiveness-of-second-language-listening-strategy-instructi/)
- Post-shadowing (after understanding) produces significant gains, but pre-shadowing does not. — [Hamada 2014, JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/node/3537)
- EL effects on TOEIC exist but are small. — [Unisma JREALL](https://riset.unisma.ac.id/index.php/JREALL/article/view/21190)

### Inferences
Proposed schedule (the researcher's synthesis, not sourced as a whole):
- Day 1: a full mock test on parroto.app (free full TOEIC mock) for a baseline and an error breakdown by part and trap type.
- Days 2 to 5, foundations: Part 1 vocabulary SRS plus being/been and sound-alike drills (about 15 min). Part 2 question-type capture: dictate first words of 30 to 50 prompts per day and shadow Q+A pairs (about 30 min). Short dictation on real videos for decoding (about 20 min).
- Days 6 to 15, Part 3/4 technique: 3 to 4 sets per day under timing with pre-reading. Then dictate the answer sentences, log paraphrase pairs to SRS and shadow one full talk (about 45 to 60 min). Keep Part 2 at 10 to 15 items per day. Add graphics and intent drills from Day 8.
- Days 16 to 26, integrated: 2 to 3 half or full mock Listening sections per week (45 min each plus 45 min review). On non-mock days, review error-log items with dictation and shadowing. Make 1 in 3 sessions UK/AU accent material.
- Days 27 to 30, taper: 1 full mock under exam conditions (Day 27 or 28) and light review of the SRS and error log. No new materials; sleep matters.
- Daily split: about 70% intensive (test audio, dictation, shadowing, review) and about 30% light extensive listening during idle time (commute, business podcasts).
